## 1. Why the ₩600M Spousal Gift Exemption Is Korea's Most Powerful Tax Shield

In South Korea, both the **Comprehensive Financial Income Tax (금융소득종합과세 - up to 49.5% marginal rate)** and the **National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) Regional Subscriber Conversion (건강보험 피부양자 탈락)** trigger the moment an individual's combined annual interest and dividend income exceeds **₩20,000,000**.

Fortunately, South Korea's Income Tax Act strictly applies **individual taxation (개인별 합산과세)**—meaning a husband and wife are treated as two completely separate tax units. If one spouse holds a ₩700M portfolio generating ₩35M in annual dividends, they breach the ₩20M cliff alone. However, by legally gifting **₩350M** of those dividend-paying equities to their spouse under the **₩600,000,000 10-year Spousal Gift Property Deduction (배우자 증여재산공제)**, each spouse now earns **₩17.5M** per year—keeping both spouses 100% below the ₩20M threshold, paying only the 15.4% flat withholding tax, and preserving NHIS health insurance dependent status!

## 3. Critical 2026 Rules: Stock Valuation Window & 1-Year Carryover Taxation

When gifting listed stocks (such as US stocks `NVDA`, `AAPL`, `SCHD` or Korean stocks) rather than cash, two strict statutory rules govern your tax filing:
1. **The 4-Month Average Closing Price Rule (전후 2개월 종가 평균):**
   Unlike cash, the gift value of a listed stock is **not** its price on the transfer date! Under Article 63 of the Inheritance and Gift Tax Act, the National Tax Service values gifted listed equities using the **arithmetic average of daily closing prices for 2 months before and 2 months after the gift transfer date (4 months total)**. Therefore, you cannot compute the exact final gift value on the day you transfer the shares—you must wait 2 months after the transfer date, calculate the 4-month average price, and file before the 3-month statutory deadline (the last day of the month falling 3 months after the gift month).
2. **The 1-Year Carryover Taxation Rule (주식 이월과세 1년 의무보유):**
   Effective since January 1, 2025, if you gift appreciated US stocks to your spouse to step up the cost basis and wipe out the 22% overseas capital gains tax, **your spouse must hold those gifted shares for at least 1 year (365 days)** before selling. If your spouse sells within 1 year of the gift date, the cost basis reverts to your original acquisition price (이월과세 적용), and furthermore, **your spouse cannot sell the proceeds back or wire the cash back to you**.

## 5. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Q1. If the gift amount is under ₩600M and payable tax is ₩0, do I really need to file on HomeTax?
For simple bank transfers used for everyday living expenses, no. However, if your spouse uses the gifted cash to **buy real estate, invest in stocks, or pay off a mortgage**, filing a formal ₩0 Gift Tax Return on HomeTax creates an official **Source of Funds Record (자금출처 소명자료)** with the National Tax Service. Furthermore, for gifted US stocks, filing the gift tax return officially locks in the **stepped-up acquisition cost basis** so your brokerage and the NTS recognize the new higher cost basis when your spouse eventually sells after 1 year!

### Q2. Does the ₩600M Spousal Gift Exemption apply if one or both spouses are foreign nationals?
**Yes, provided the receiving spouse (수증자 - Donee) is a Tax Resident (거주자) of South Korea.** Under the Inheritance and Gift Tax Act, if the receiving spouse is a **Non-Resident (비거주자)** for tax purposes, they are **ineligible** for the ₩600M spousal deduction! Always verify that the receiving spouse satisfies the Korean tax residency test (domicile or 183+ days residence in Korea) before executing large asset transfers.

### Q3. Are transfers for household living expenses or children's tuition counted toward the ₩600M limit?
**No.** Under Article 46 of the Inheritance and Gift Tax Act, socially customary transfers for **household living expenses (생활비), medical bills, and education tuition** are 100% tax-exempt and do **not** consume any of your ₩600M spousal limit—**unless** the receiving spouse saves those living-expense transfers into time deposits, buys stocks, or purchases real estate with them.
